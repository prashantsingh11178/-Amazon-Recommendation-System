Which recommender works best for books?

I compared four ways of recommending books (popularity, item-based collaborative filtering, matrix factorisation and content-based) plus a hybrid, using the Book-Crossing dataset, and analysed how Amazon’s recommender keeps users engaged.

Dataset

9,439books

Book-Crossing stores a rating of 0 when someone interacted with a book but did not rate it. Those 716,109 rows were dropped, so only real ratings were used. Only users and books with at least 5 ratings were kept. Split: 80% train, 20% test per user; a book counts as “liked” at rating 8 or higher.

The models

Baseline

Popularity

Recommends the most-rated books to everyone. No personalisation.

Collaborative

Item-kNN

Finds books whose raters overlap with books you liked (cosine similarity with shrinkage).

Collaborative

MF-ALS

Learns hidden taste factors for users and books with alternating least squares, on top of user and book biases.

Content

Content-based

TF-IDF on title, author and publisher builds a taste profile from what you rated.

Hybrid

Hybrid blend

Weighted mix of the other models’ standardised scores.

How Amazon keeps users engaged

Amazon’s system relies on item-to-item collaborative filtering: similarities between products are computed offline, then looked up in milliseconds when a page loads. That choice is what lets it scale to a huge catalogue. These diagrams come from my Task 1 report; they are a simplified view based on public papers, not Amazon’s internal design.

Takeaways

  • Personalisation beats popularity. Item-kNN, Content-based and Hybrid rank about 2 to 3 times better than the popularity baseline, and cover over 80% of the catalogue while popularity covers 0.2%.
  • The hybrid wins overall, with the lowest error and the best NDCG@10 and HitRate@10.
  • Low rating error does not mean a good top-10 list. MF-ALS predicts ratings better than popularity and Item-kNN but ranks books poorly.
  • Content features help on sparse data, since they need no overlap between users.
  • Limits: one random split, only explicit ratings used, and cold-start users or books were not evaluated.
